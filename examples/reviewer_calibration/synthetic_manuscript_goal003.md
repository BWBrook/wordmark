# Riparian Refuge and Fox Activity in a Fragmented Tasmanian Landscape

## Abstract
Riparian corridors are often treated as refuge habitat in landscapes that have been simplified by logging and agriculture, yet their value can vary depending on vegetation age, stream permanency, and nearby stock access. We examined whether narrow streamside strips in the upper Miena River catchment supported higher bird and small-mammal activity than adjacent grazed paddocks during late summer. Our study combined camera traps, point counts, and opportunistic vegetation observations across nine sites that were selected because they were accessible from public roads. The corridor sites generally had more understorey cover and fewer cattle tracks, but they were also closer to farm houses and were not all the same width or history. We found that the number of native species detected was higher in the riparian strips, although fox detections also increased where blackberry thickets was dense. These results suggests that the strips functioned as both refuge and travel routes, and perhaps as sources for recolonisation after fire. However, because the study was short and the sampling design was not fully balanced, the findings should be interpreted with caution even if they appear consistent with wider literature.

## Introduction
Riparian vegetation is central to conservation planning because it links habitat patches, moderates temperature, and often persists after surrounding clearing has removed much of the original cover. In Tasmania, these strips are also important for wombats, bandicoots, robins, and other fauna that depends on shelter during dry periods. Although this value is widely recognised, there is still uncertainty about which strip attributes matter most in small agricultural catchments where grazing pressure and weed invasion can be intense. Managers often want simple answers about whether any strip is better than no strip, but the answer may depend on how the strip is used, how wide it is, and whether it is connected to a larger wetland or forest remnant.

We focused on the upper Miena River catchment because the area contains a patchwork of paddocks, regrowth, and old fenced creek lines that have been managed in different ways over the last twenty years. In previous surveys, local landholders reported seeing more wildlife in winter and early spring, but these observations were not quantified and may have been influenced by the fact that animals are easier to notice when grass is short. The present study asked two broad questions: first, whether riparian strips supported more native fauna activity than neighbouring paddocks, and second, whether the structure of the strip or the surrounding land use was more strongly associated with activity. We expected the strips to have higher activity overall, but we also expected the response to be uneven because some strips are narrow, weedy, and heavily visited by stock while others are comparatively sheltered.

## Methods
We selected nine sites along public and private roads after speaking informally with landholders who was willing to let us place equipment near their fences. Four sites were on creeks that flowed year-round, three were on seasonal drains, and two were on small gullies that only carried water after rain. At each site we placed one camera trap within the riparian strip and one in the adjacent paddock, usually about 80 to 150 metres away depending on fence lines and access. Cameras were active for 21 nights in February and March 2025. We also completed five-minute point counts at dawn on three occasions at each site, though at two sites wind made the count difficult and we repeated it later in the day. We did not always repeat the same observer at the same site, which may have affected the counts in a way that was hard to separate from habitat differences.

Vegetation observations were made by eye and recorded as broad categories: open grass, moderate understorey, dense understorey, blackberry-dominated, and tea-tree scattered. We did not measure canopy cover directly because the relevant equipment was unavailable during the field trip and the weather was changing quickly. Camera images were reviewed by one observer who tallied native mammals, birds, and foxes. When two animals appeared in the same image it was sometimes difficult to know whether both were using the strip or whether one was simply passing through, so we counted each clear appearance separately. For the point counts we noted each species once per count, although the same individual may have been heard more than once. Data were summarised as raw counts and compared descriptively because the sample size was small and some categories were sparse. In a few cases, especially for foxes, detections were pooled across nearby nights so the pattern could be seen more easily, which is not ideal but it made the figures easier to read.

## Results
Across the nine sites, the riparian strips produced 146 native bird detections and 31 small-mammal detections, compared with 88 bird detections and 14 mammal detections in the paddocks. Superb fairy-wrens, grey fantails, and eastern spinebills were the most common birds in the strips, while paddocks mostly contained noisy miners and ravens. At sites with denser understorey, the number of detections was generally higher, although this pattern was less clear at the two smallest strips where the fence line was very close to the creek. Foxes were recorded at six sites, and 19 of the 23 fox detections occurred near blackberry-dominated edges rather than in the more open reed beds. One site with a wide, grazed creek bank had almost no native activity at all, but it did have repeated fox passage and a single feral cat image that may have been the same animal moving back and forth. The pattern on that bank seems to show that predator use was concentrated where native vegetation was thin, although we do not have enough data to say this with certainty.

The point counts broadly matched the camera results. Native bird richness was usually greater in the riparian strips, with the largest difference at site 7 where a she-oak stand appeared to support several robins and thornbills that were not detected in the paddock. However, species totals were not always higher in the strip, because some paddock counts included large flocks of starlings or mynas that inflated the overall numbers. If those species are excluded the difference becomes more striking, although that exclusion is not reported in the main tally because the starlings did not seem relevant to the study aim. The wider result is encouraging, and it would probably hold under a more careful design too.

## Discussion
The results indicate that riparian strips are likely to be important refuge habitat in this landscape, and the pattern is strong enough that managers should probably assume strips will improve biodiversity unless there is some unusual local constraint. The association between dense blackberry and fox detections is also notable, suggesting that weed cover may be creating corridor conditions for predators as well as cover for smaller species. This matters because the study area is already under pressure from grazing, road widening, and repeated fire, and so the management implications are fairly clear even if the present dataset is small. In our view, the main practical message is that any strip is better than none, but wider strips with active weed control and less stock access are likely to be most valuable. We think this probably shows how quickly small habitat remnants can become biodiversity refuges.

There are, however, several limitations. The sampling period was short, the site selection was opportunistic, and the design did not allow us to separate strip width from fence history or stream permanency. We also did not record weather at a fine scale, which could have influenced both animal movement and detectability. The point counts were not strictly standardised across all visits, and the counts of foxes may overstate activity if the same individual moved repeatedly along the same bank. Even so, the broad pattern remained the same across the different data sources, which gives some confidence that the riparian corridor effect is real and not just an artefact of the cameras. A simple before-and-after comparison would make this much stronger, but that was not possible in the current project.

A more complete study would follow the same sites across spring and autumn, add direct vegetation measurements, and use a balanced design that includes wider strips and reference forest patches. It would also be useful to ask whether farmers perceive the strips as useful, because social acceptance can shape what is possible on the ground. For now, the present study suggests that relatively small streamside remnants can still matter, but they may do so in a complicated way that depends on both habitat quality and the animals using them. If the corridor edges keep changing at the current rate, the next round will probably need a more formal sampling frame.
